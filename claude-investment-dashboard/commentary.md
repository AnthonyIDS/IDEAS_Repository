## The view from the desk

I've watched a lot of markets, and the honest truth is that nobody can reliably call the next six weeks, including me. This year has already had a sharp tech sell-off in the spring, an oil spike on Middle East news, and a recovery that surprised plenty of people who sold. None of that changed what a sensible portfolio should look like.

So my market view is short: stay invested, keep costs low, own a bit of everything, and let time do the heavy lifting. A process you can stick with beats a prediction you can't.

## How to rebalance (and how not to)

- **Once a year, on a date you pick and keep,** look at the weights. If something has wandered, trim what grew and add to what shrank.
- **Between annual reviews, act only when a holding drifts more than 5 percentage points from its target.** The risk panel above will flag it. Smaller wiggles are just noise.
- **Use new money first.** Directing fresh contributions to the underweight holdings rebalances without selling, which saves taxes and trading costs.
- **In taxable accounts, mind the tax bill.** Sometimes waiting a few weeks to reach a long-term holding period is worth more than perfect weights.
- **Never rebalance because of a headline.** Rebalance because the numbers crossed a line you drew in advance.

## Habits that matter more than picks

- **Don't sell in a panic.** The worst days and the best days tend to arrive close together. Investors who bail out after a drop often miss the rebound.
- **Ignore most of the noise.** Daily price moves, cable-news countdowns and social-media "can't miss" calls are entertainment. Check this dashboard as often as you like, but act rarely.
- **Resist the hot stock.** When everyone at the barbecue owns the same thing, the easy money has usually been made. Our 8% cap on any single stock exists precisely so excitement can't take over the portfolio.
- **Write down why you own each thing.** The thesis column in the holdings table is there so you can ask, calmly, "Is the reason still true?" A lower price alone isn't a reason to sell. A broken thesis is.

## What could go wrong

I'd be doing you a disservice if I didn't spell this out.

- **Technology concentration hides inside the index fund.** VTI is about 41% technology by Vanguard's classification, and we also own Microsoft directly. A long tech bear market would hurt more than the sector table suggests at first glance. We keep an eye on it in the risk panel.
- **Bonds aren't a guaranteed cushion.** In 2022, stocks and bonds fell together. If inflation re-accelerates, BND can lose money at the same time as equities.
- **Each stock carries a real company risk.** Union Pacific's merger may be blocked or conditioned. NextEra's Dominion deal adds debt and regulatory risk. J&J's talc settlement could fall apart. Shell lives and dies with oil prices. Berkshire is learning to live without its founder. Any of these could go wrong for years.
- **International stocks can lag for a decade.** They have before. Owning them is insurance, and insurance sometimes feels like wasted money.
- **The whole portfolio can fall 30% or more in a bad bear market.** With roughly 82% in stocks, that's not a tail risk. It's something you should expect to live through at least once. If that would make you sell, hold more bonds.

## About those short-term forecasts

The Wall Street Research Outlook above is a research tool, not a trading signal. Its 2-, 4- and 6-week numbers are modeled scenarios built from each stock's own volatility, scheduled events and published research. They'll often be wrong, and the scorecard will show exactly how wrong.

Use them to ask better questions. Is an earnings date coming up? Is the range unusually wide? Has the research tone shifted? Then go back to the plan. A six-week guess should never talk you out of a twenty-year strategy. If a forecast makes you want to abandon the allocation, that's a signal to step away from the screen, not to trade.

_Patience is a position._
